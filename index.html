Where the Story Begins

Cameroon — often called "Africa in Miniature"

Before Wouro Yobi, there is Cameroon. The country earns its nickname honestly: within its borders sit coastline, rainforest, savanna, highland plateau and near-desert, along with more than 250 living languages. It borders Nigeria, Chad, the Central African Republic, the Republic of the Congo, Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, sitting at the hinge between West and Central Africa.

That geographic range matters for this archive, because Wouro Yobi's own landscape — savanna grassland on a highland plateau — is just one part of a much larger and more varied national picture.

One Region, Many Peoples

The Adamawa Region

The Adamawa Plateau rises across north-central Cameroon, spilling into neighbouring Nigeria and the Central African Republic — a highland band of rolling savanna, cooler air and grassland that divides the country's forested south from its drier north. Cattle-based pastoralism dominates the regional economy, alongside subsistence farming.

The region is home to several peoples, not one: the Fulbe (Fulani) form the largest group, but Mbum, Gbaya, Tikar, Mambila, Vute and other communities share the plateau, each with their own history and language. Traditional Muslim rulers known as lamidos hold significant local authority across many Adamawa towns, a structure with roots in the 19th-century Adamawa Emirate.

This archive documents Wouro Yobi specifically. The Adamawa Region's wider diversity — its many peoples, towns and traditions — is referenced here for context, not as a description of any single community within it.

A People, Not a Single Story

The Mbororo story

The Mbororo are the historically nomadic, pastoral branch of the wider Fulani (Fulɓe) people — among the largest nomadic pastoral populations in the world, found across the Sahel from Senegal to Sudan. In Cameroon and neighbouring countries, Mbororo herders speak Fulfulde and have traditionally moved cattle across the landscape in search of water and pasture through the seasons.

Much of Mbororo life is shaped by pulaaku — a code of conduct emphasising patience, dignity, hospitality and restraint — passed down rather than written, and by deep working knowledge of livestock, land and weather built up over generations. Mbororo communities are not uniform: they differ across regions and countries in dialect, custom and degree of settlement, and organisations such as MBOSCUDA now represent Mbororo interests in advocacy, education and land rights across Cameroon.

General Mbororo history, above, is drawn from public scholarship and reference sources. Wouro Yobi's own particular history, leadership and traditions are documented separately in Our Identity — the two should not be read as interchangeable.
